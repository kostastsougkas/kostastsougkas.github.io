A short introduction

What are fractals?

There is no single definition that captures every object mathematicians call a fractal. People often describe fractals as shapes whose detail continues to appear at smaller and smaller scales.

Many fractals are self-similar: they are made from smaller, scaled copies of themselves. Repeating a simple rule can produce intricate geometry. Many fractals have a non-integer dimension, although not every fractal is self-similar or has a non-integer dimension.

A quick idea of dimension

What does dimension measure?

Dimension is one way of describing how the amount of detail in a shape changes with scale. For an exactly self-similar shape, the similarity dimension compares the number of smaller copies with their size relative to the whole.

If the whole consists of N smaller copies of itself, each with linear scale factor 1/r, its similarity dimension d satisfies N = rd. Equivalently, d = log(N) / log(r).

The gasket's dimension lies between 1 and 2. Although it is more intricate than a line, its two-dimensional area is exactly zero: after n construction steps, the remaining area is (3/4)n times the area of the original triangle, and this tends to zero as n increases. Its two-dimensional Lebesgue measure is therefore zero. Hausdorff measure generalises length and area to arbitrary dimensions, including non-integer ones. At the gasket's dimension, d = log(3) / log(2), its d-dimensional Hausdorff measure is finite and positive.
